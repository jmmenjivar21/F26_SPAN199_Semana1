¡Bienvenidos! · Welcome

Your first words with a Spanish-speaking patient

Every patient encounter starts the same way: a greeting, a name, and a question. In this chapter you'll learn to pronounce Spanish clearly, greet a patient at any hour, introduce yourself professionally, and ask the two questions that open almost every intake: ¿Cómo se llama usted? and ¿De dónde es usted? You'll also learn when to use usted and when to use tú — a choice that signals respect in a clinical setting.

What this chapter covers

Eight building blocks that let you open a conversation in Spanish with confidence. Everything is graded automatically, so you can see exactly which pieces still need work before class.

Nota cultural · Respect in the clinic

El respeto: usted, don y doña

In most Spanish-speaking cultures, addressing an adult patient with usted is a basic sign of respect — especially with older adults. Many patients also expect titles: señor, señora, don, doña. Using tú with an adult you have just met can come across as dismissive, even when you mean to be warm. When in doubt in a healthcare setting, always start with usted and let the patient invite you to switch.

¡OJO! · letras difíciles

h is always silent (hola = "ola"). j sounds like English "h" (Juan). ll sounds like "y" (ella). ñ sounds like "ny" (mañana). rr is trilled (perro). qu = "k" (qué). g before e/i sounds like "h" (gente).

G2Las cinco vocales

Spanish vowels are short, pure, and never change. Master these five and your pronunciation is already clear.

Aahfather
Eehmet

Oohmore

Practice with names you'll hear in clinic: M-a-r-í-a (ma-RI-a) · J-o-s-é (ho-SE) · L-u-i-s (LWEES) · C-a-r-m-e-n (KAR-men) · M-e-n-j-í-v-a-r (men-HI-var).

CLAVE · sin diptongos ingleses

English speakers tend to glide vowels ("o" → "ow"). In Spanish keep each vowel pure and short. No is "noh," not "nooow." This one habit makes you far easier for patients to understand.

G4Pronombres personales · subject pronouns

Who is doing the action. Spanish often drops them, because the verb ending already tells you.

SingularPlural
1st personyo — Inosotros / nosotras — we
2nd person (informal)tú — youustedes — you all
2nd person (formal)usted — youustedes — you all
3rd personél — he · ella — sheellos / ellas — they

Abbreviations on forms and charts: usted → Ud. · ustedes → Uds.

Dropping the pronoun: Yo soy enfermera and Soy enfermera both mean "I am a nurse." Use the pronoun for emphasis or contrast: Yo soy el enfermero; él es el doctor.

¡OJO! · usted is grammatically "he/she"

Even though usted means "you," it takes the same verb form as él/ella: usted es, usted se llama. This trips up nearly every beginner. In Latin America, ustedes covers both formal and informal "you all" — vosotros is used mainly in Spain.

¡OJO! · sin artículo

With professions, Spanish drops the article: Soy enfermera — not "soy una enfermera." Add de for origin: Soy de Guatemala.

G6El verbo LLAMARSE · reflexivo

Literally "to call oneself." The reflexive pronoun changes with the subject, and the verb itself is regular -ar.

— ¿Cómo se llama usted? (What is your name? — formal)

— Me llamo Roberto Díaz. (My name is Roberto Díaz.)

— Mucho gusto. Yo me llamo Ana, soy su enfermera. (Nice to meet you. I'm Ana, I'm your nurse.)

DOS APELLIDOS

Most Spanish speakers carry two last names: father's first, then mother's. Roberto Díaz Hernández is filed under Díaz, not Hernández. On intake forms, always confirm: ¿Cuál es su primer apellido? This prevents a very common charting error.

PALABRAS INTERROGATIVAS

¿qué? what · ¿quién? who · ¿dónde? where · ¿cuándo? when · ¿cómo? how · ¿cuántos? how many · ¿cuál? which. Every question word carries an accent, and questions open with ¿.
